Coin Cavern is out: one ring, 125,000 coins and a machine

November 16, 2026 · by Dr.Dev

Coin Cavern went live on November 15. The idea fits in one sentence: a room 10 feet deep in coins, and somewhere in it, one lost ring. You dig, you haul the coins to a sorting machine, the machine sells them, and you spend the money on ways to dig faster. Here's how it came together.

Why a machine instead of an x-ray

The first rule I wrote down was: no x-ray, no metal detector. Nobody knows where the ring is, and the game shouldn't either. A detector would turn the whole thing into "walk to the beep". Without one, the only way to find the ring is the honest way, coin by coin.

That raised a problem. If you're going to move a whole room of coins, moving them has to be fun to watch. So the coins don't just disappear into a bank. They go into a machine I kept adding to long after it was sensible: a feeder wheel, zig-zag rails, a weigh scale, polish brushes that send coins in dull and out shiny, a glass loop-the-loop, a bucket elevator, a plinko board, a counting wheel, a magnet drum that pulls out the blue coins, and finally four clear collectors. Every coin takes 8 seconds to make the trip, and it's paid for when it lands, so you watch your money arrive.

The machine is also the reason the game has a shape. Your digging, your bin, the hopper and the sorter are a chain, and the slowest link sets your pace. Early on you're short on scoop. Later the hopper backs up and you need a faster sorter. Then the collectors overflow and you need a coin wrapper. Every upgrade fixes one bottleneck and shows you the next one.

What went live

Each room holds about 125,000 coins in four kinds: pennies, silver, blue and gold, all mixed together. You start with your bare hands and a 40-coin tub, and work up to a shovel that moves 400 coins at a time. The shop already had a pulley line, a second bin, a conveyor chute, an apprentice who digs on their own, a dustpan dwarf who sweeps up spilled coins, a coin wrapper, polish and a small bank that pays interest. Every room also hid odd items for a photo book, each with a finder's bonus, and look-alike rings that differ from the real one in a single detail: a brass band instead of gold, a red stone instead of blue, "Forever" engraved instead of "Always".

There was also a short tutorial that walks you through the first scoop, the first bin, the first sale and the first upgrade, with a "Skip tutorial" button on every step. The first iPad test that night found the coach card sitting on top of the very button it pointed at, so it learned to move out of the way.

The first two days

The biggest change came that same evening. At launch, the ring rode the machine like everything else, and you checked it in the tray. That made where the ring happened to be buried decide how long a room took, and the find itself happened off to the side, in the tray. Now the ring always turns up in the room's last 5,000 coins, at a point picked at random when the room starts, and your own scoop is what finds it. There's slow motion, fireworks, and a rubber stamp that slams down MISSION ACCOMPLISHED. Then the coins drain away to show a painting on the floor of the coin box. A steady player finds the ring in about 15 to 20 minutes.

That night the game also got a story. You work for Lost & Found Co., and every room has a client with something to find. Room 1 is Grandma June's wedding ring, in Grandma's Coin Closet. Room 2 is Bea Butterfield's first dollar, lost in the Penny Parlor after a customer sneezed. The other eight rooms have a marble, a token, a sock, some car keys and more, and a postcard from someone called "M." comes with each one.

On November 16 the game moved to its own address, coincavern.sideeffectsstudio.buzz. The first two rooms got their own machines: Grandma's closet sorts coins with tape-measure rails, a thimble lift and a music box, and the Penny Parlor has the Spoon Cascade, with seesaw spoons, a Ferris wheel of ice-cream cones and a chain reaction of wafer dominoes. And the look-alikes got a moment of their own. When one lands, a sad trombone plays and a stamp prints FAKE! with what was wrong.

What's next

The next step is a proper test on real phones: pinch zoom, dragging the bin, and whether everything fits on a small screen. After that I want a landscape layout for phones. A few machine ideas are on my list too, like a ticker-tape printer and a different bell for each coin, but none of that is promised yet. If you play, I'd love to know how long your first ring took.
